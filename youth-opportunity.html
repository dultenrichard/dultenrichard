How to evaluate investment in youth programs

Dulten Richard · November 27, 2026 · AI-assisted editorial draft, prepared for publication; author review pending.

A practical framework for assessing youth programming, access and public accountability.

When a youth program is reviewed, a budget number alone tells us very little about the experience available to participants. A decision about funding is also a decision about what activities can be offered, who can attend, how regularly they run and whether staff can deliver them safely.

My involvement in cadets and Bring Back Cadets makes these questions particularly relevant to me. They apply to many other programs, too: sports, community arts, mentoring and civic education. Each has different costs and different intended outcomes.

A useful evaluation should start with access. How many young people can participate, and what prevents others from joining? A free program may still be inaccessible if transportation, scheduling or equipment creates barriers. The next question is continuity: are opportunities predictable enough for participants and families to plan around them?

Quality also matters. Programs should be able to explain the training or activities they deliver, how staff are prepared, and what safeguards and reporting routes exist. Participant feedback should be considered alongside attendance and completion data. Neither a testimonial nor a spreadsheet is enough on its own.

Finally, the public should be able to understand what a funding decision changes. An announcement of additional resources is not the same thing as confirmation that a specific activity has resumed. Clear timelines, transparent eligibility rules and follow-up reporting make it possible to distinguish a commitment from delivery.

These are evaluation questions rather than claims that any single program has achieved a particular financial return. The case for public investment is stronger when evidence, competing priorities and outcomes can be discussed openly.
